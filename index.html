The daily 15 minutes

  • 1. Warm-up — straw + lip trills (5 min). Best-evidenced tone exercise there is.
  • 2. Pitch work — 10 matches, then 5 blind matches (5 min). The blind reps are the ones that stick.
  • 3. Repertoire — one phrase of a real song against its reference, three takes, listen to your own recording between takes (5 min).
  • Weekly: one voice report to watch the numbers move. Daily shorts beat weekend marathons (spacing effect, d≈0.54).

Why this design — the science in one screen

  • Seeing your pitch in real time works. Controlled studies from the SINGAD classroom experiments (Welch 1989) through Wilson et al. 2008 and Berglin et al. 2022 show visual pitch feedback beats instruction alone — in Berglin’s adult study, only the visual-feedback group improved significantly.
  • …but you must also practice without the screen. Motor-learning research (the “guidance hypothesis”) shows constant feedback inflates performance while it’s on and fades when removed. Hence the blind match mode: sing first, see the trace after.
  • “Tone-deafness” is almost never the problem. Only ~1.5–4% of people have a true perceptual deficit; most off-pitch singing is a voice-control mapping issue (Hutchins & Peretz 2012) — which trains quickly, often within 4–8 short sessions.
  • Match voices, in your range. People match pitch better against voice-like sounds in their own comfortable range than piano notes in the wrong octave (Hutchins et al. 2014; Pfordresher & Greenspon 2025). The exercises calibrate to your range and use a harmonic-rich tone.
  • Resonance is measurable. Trained voices carry 10–20 dB more energy near 2.4–3.4 kHz — the “singer’s formant” (Sundberg 1974). The report’s ring metric (singing power ratio, Omori 1996) tracks exactly this, and it correlates with expert quality ratings.
  • Straw phonation is the tone exercise with the best evidence. It measurably reshapes the vocal tract toward the ringing configuration and lowers phonation effort (Titze 2006; Guzman 2013 CT study; 2021 systematic review).
  • Record yourself and listen. You literally cannot hear your own voice accurately while singing (bone conduction); playback closes the gap and improves self-assessment (Silveira & Gavin 2016).

Phone setup for good measurements

  • Quiet room, phone ~30 cm from your mouth (about a forearm).
  • No Bluetooth headsets — iOS drops the mic to phone-call quality. Built-in mic or wired headphones only.
  • iOS applies some automatic gain no matter what, so the app measures pitch and spectrum shape (which survive it), not absolute loudness.
  • If audio stops after a call or screen lock, tap anywhere — the page resumes the audio engine on the next touch.
