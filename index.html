The daily 15 minutes

  • 1. Warm-up — straw + lip trills (5 min). Best-evidenced tone exercise there is.
  • 2. Pitch work — 10 matches, then 5 blind matches (5 min). The blind reps are the ones that stick. If the report flagged your resonance rather than your pitch, spend this block on the Ring trainer instead.
  • 3. Repertoire — one phrase of a real song against its reference, three takes, listen to your own recording between takes (5 min).
  • Weekly: one voice report to watch the numbers move. Daily shorts beat weekend marathons (spacing effect, d≈0.54).

Why this design — the science in one screen

  • Seeing your pitch in real time works. Controlled studies from the SINGAD classroom experiments (Welch 1989) through Wilson et al. 2008 and Berglin et al. 2022 show visual pitch feedback beats instruction alone — in Berglin’s adult study, only the visual-feedback group improved significantly.
  • …but you must also practice without the screen. Motor-learning research (the “guidance hypothesis”) shows constant feedback inflates performance while it’s on and fades when removed. Hence the blind match mode: sing first, see the trace after.
  • “Tone-deafness” is almost never the problem. Only ~1.5–4% of people have a true perceptual deficit; most off-pitch singing is a voice-control mapping issue (Hutchins & Peretz 2012) — which trains quickly, often within 4–8 short sessions.
  • Match voices, in your range. People match pitch better against voice-like sounds in their own comfortable range than piano notes in the wrong octave (Hutchins et al. 2014; Pfordresher & Greenspon 2025). The exercises calibrate to your range and use a harmonic-rich tone.
  • Resonance is measurable. Trained voices carry 10–20 dB more energy near 2.4–3.4 kHz — the “singer’s formant” (Sundberg 1974). The report’s ring metric (singing power ratio, Omori 1996) tracks exactly this, and it correlates with expert quality ratings.
  • Straw phonation is the tone exercise with the best evidence. It measurably reshapes the vocal tract toward the ringing configuration and lowers phonation effort (Titze 2006; Guzman 2013 CT study; 2021 systematic review).
  • Record yourself and listen. You literally cannot hear your own voice accurately while singing (bone conduction); playback closes the gap and improves self-assessment (Silveira & Gavin 2016).

Full citations and thresholds: RESEARCH.md.

Phone setup for good measurements

  • Quiet room, phone ~30 cm from your mouth (about a forearm).
  • No Bluetooth headsets — iOS drops the mic to phone-call quality. Built-in mic or wired headphones only.
  • iOS applies some automatic gain no matter what, so the app measures pitch and spectrum shape (which survive it), not absolute loudness.
  • If audio stops after a call or screen lock, tap anywhere — the page resumes the audio engine on the next touch.

Why it’s built this way

Why the meter goes away. Steinhauer & Eichhorn (2025) taught 92 adults this exact skill — twang — and crossed practice structure with feedback frequency. Blocked practice with feedback on every rep won the in-session scores and degraded what was retained; random practice with feedback on ~55% of reps did the opposite. So stages 1–2 give you the meter to find the sensation, and stages 3–4 take it away, because that is the part that lasts.

Why these vowels, in this order. Singer’s-formant energy is vowel-dependent (Feng & Howard 2023), so the ladder runs ring-easy to ring-hard and re-baselines each vowel on its first appearance — otherwise the ladder would just be measuring vowel changes and calling it progress.

Twang narrows the epilaryngeal tube and boosts 2–4 kHz (Titze et al. 2003; Lombard & Steinhauer 2007; Jelinger et al. 2024 MRI). Real-time singer’s-formant feedback goes back to Rossiter & Howard’s ALBERT (1996). Citations: RESEARCH.md.

SOVT warm-up

Semi-occluded vocal tract exercises — phonating through a straw or trilled lips — are the best-evidenced tone exercise in voice science (Titze 2006; CT-imaging and trial evidence in RESEARCH.md). They lower the effort needed to make sound and nudge the throat into the shape that produces “ring.” You need a thin drinking straw (a stirring straw is even better).

Prove it to yourself

Sing it in style

The same acoustic move — a scoop, a breathy verse, a late vibrato, a register flip — is an error in classical scoring and a virtue in R&B. Pick the sound you’re chasing; the drills below train the R&B toolkit specifically. Background and citations: STYLE-GUIDE.md.

🏃 Runs & riffs trainer

R&B runs live on the pentatonic scale, mostly descending, landing on a chord tone. Method with the best support: hear it → sing it slow → speed ladder. Notes need only land within ±50¢ mid-run (fast pitch is perceived categorically), but the last note is the anchor — that one’s scored tight.

Speed: notes/sec (start at 2–3, earn your way up)

🛝 Scoop control

An intentional R&B scoop is ~100–300¢ deep, takes 80–250 ms, and lands stable. A slide that never settles is just flat singing. You’ll hear a target note — start clearly below it, slide in, then hold it dead straight.

🌊 Delayed vibrato

Classical vibrato spins from the first instant; R&B holds the note straight and lets vibrato bloom in the final stretch (>40% in). Hold one note ~4 s: straight first, then release into vibrato.

🎚️ Breathiness dial

R&B lives on the contrast — airy and intimate in the verse, closed and present in the chorus. The skill isn’t being breathy or being clear, it’s commanding both on demand. Sing the same “ah” twice, one setting each, on the same note; this measures how far apart your two settings genuinely are.

It reads H1–H2, the amplitude of your first harmonic minus your second — a direct read on how firmly the vocal folds are closing, independent of how bright your throat makes the sound. Absolute values vary hugely between people and are skewed by phone-mic bass rolloff, so only the gap between your two takes is reported: in a contrast, all of that cancels.
